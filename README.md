<h1 align="center">Isometric Town</h1>

<p align="center">Pick any town and draw it in isometric 3D, as plotter-ready SVG linework.</p>

<p align="center"><b><a href="https://isometrictown.com">isometrictown.com</a></b></p>

Search for a place, drag the little map to frame it, and turn the view. Isometric Town fetches the buildings, streets, water and parks from OpenStreetMap and draws them as an isometric 3D scene. You can download the result as an SVG with one layer per pen.

## What it draws

- Image towns reserve paved squares for compact sites inside built-up neighbourhoods, up to 2,500 m². Image towns can gather shops along a well-connected street near the centre. Where the picture admits several land uses, nearby homes favour a park and nearby workshops favour an industrial district. Raising **Likeness** reduces both preferences.
- European-style image towns seek a parish church among established homes where the picture can accommodate its nave and steeple. Image towns can run a street tram along a broad avenue through the centre. Village passenger lines follow broad curves around obstacles, with spaced level crossings. A station can terminate a branch or serve a through line alongside a street, with a station building and covered platform. Image towns can have bus shelters along main streets, petrol stations in industrial districts, fire stations and hospitals, and small places of worship among the terraces. Car parks have access aisles and marked bays. Detached houses with room behind them can have narrow lap pools, ovals and round-cornered pools. Small lakes can be empty or carry two or three sailing boats where there is room. At most one lake in an image town gets a ferry, with paths from two different roads to piers on facing banks and open water across. Park paths and bordering streets can lead to playgrounds, tennis or basketball courts, and oval running tracks where the park has room. Gabled barns with room for silos stand beside rural roads and tracks. Farm tracks divide fields, orchards and vineyards, and some meadow tracks serve campsites. Open meadows can hold boulders and scree, with occasional terraced quarries beside roads or farm tracks. Grassy shores grow marsh reeds and woodland edges can give way to scrub.
- In image towns, compact houses can face the street gable-end first. Paired factory halls can share a central link with loading courts at both ends. A roomy courtyard among terraces can hold a freestanding castle keep.
- Image-town buildings take their plans from 60 real places, learnt from map data: L- and T-shaped houses, porch recesses and notched corners in the suburbs, terraces with rear wings and stair towers reaching into their yards, often repeated along a street and mirrored in pairs, and stepped halls and towers. At coarse scales, where such detail would not show, buildings stay plain boxes.
- **Neighbourhoods as in real towns** in the Image panel arranges districts the way they border each other in 60 real places, learnt from map data: works on the water, suburbs out by the fields, towers among blocks and squares. At 0% the town keeps its hand-made zoning.
- **Height from image** in the Image panel lets a picture shape the skyline. At 0% each neighbourhood keeps its usual building heights. Turn it up for low buildings in pale areas, rising through mid-rise blocks to towers in the darkest patches.
- **Building density** sets overall plot coverage in image mode. Lower it to leave more gardens and gaps, or raise it to extend buildings where their plots have room. **Building density from image** below zero thins buildings across the picture, especially in pale areas. Above zero it fills more of each dark block with buildings and leaves gardens and gaps in pale areas. **Hatching from image** changes the spacing of lines on roofs, walls, fields, parks and cast shadows. Use these controls independently to shape the town or change how much ink it lays down.
- **Countryside ↔ town** favours open land or buildings where both match the image. **Water preference** favours drier or wetter ground. **District size** controls how readily small patches merge, and **Street spacing** changes the size of blocks within built districts. All four can be adjusted inside masks.
- **Streets follow image lines** draws roads and paths along bright centrelines and long, connected edges in the picture. At zero, both forms of tracing are off. **House floorplans follow image lines** turns detached houses and draws the backs of terraces towards nearby sustained contours. **Preserve empty space** leaves transparent areas as bare paper, including holes inside a logo. Lower its **Highlight cutoff** to leave the lightest parts of an opaque picture blank too.
- **Masks** in the Image panel set some settings differently in part of the picture, as adjustment masks do in a photo editor. A mask is an ellipse, a rectangle or a polygon drawn over the preview: drag it, size and turn it by its handles, pull new corners out of a polygon's sides and round its corners. Inside it the mask's own Brightness, Contrast, Likeness, Neighbourhoods, Street pattern, Streets follow image lines, More roundabouts, Height from image, Building density, Building density from image, Hatching, House floorplans, Preserve empty space, Building height, Pitched roofs, Trees, Traffic, Abandonment and Humanize apply, and Windows, Roof hatching, Rooftop boxes, Roof details and Shade walls can be turned on or off there. Outside it the drawing's own apply, and across its feathered edge the two mix. Masks can be inverted, weakened, hidden, duplicated, mirrored across the picture and reordered; later ones mix over earlier ones. Choose **Add source tint** to blend a picked colour into the image under a mask. Its blend mode and **Tint amount** guide the town’s layout. The image well and Image overlay show the tinted source.
- **Pins** put things from the catalogue where you want them, and the town is laid out round them. Pick a kind from Add a pin, or choose Pin one in its catalogue entry, then drag its symbol over the preview. As with masks, a pin shows only while it is selected in the list. A place of worship, a town hall, a school or a hospital takes the open lot nearest its pin that it fits on, or, where there is none (along terraced rows, out in the country), a site of its own cleared beside the nearest street, with a path to it. A fire station takes the nearest building of a size for one, and a roundabout the nearest junction. The town's own landmarks keep clear of pinned ones, so a pinned town hall is the only one. A park, wood, pond, square, fields or allotments takes the ground under its circle, following the picture's edges; its Feather softens the circle's edge, so the picture decides where a wood thins out. Where nothing near a pin has room, it is left out and marked *no room*. Symbols show only over the preview, never in the SVG or the plot.

- **Humanize** loosens the drawing from precise linework to a hand sketch, with uneven strokes and early or late starts and stops. Zero keeps the original drawing. The frame and map notes stay precise.

- **Buildings** use their real footprints and heights from the map data. Most buildings have no height in the map, so their storeys are worked out from what they are, the region and how densely built the town is round them: low houses in suburbs, taller rows and blocks in town centres.
- **Windows** are set by floor in a pattern that suits the building and the region: plain grids, pairs, tall French windows, small deep ones, piecemeal ones on houses, ribbon windows on modern blocks, curtain walls on towers, round-headed windows on churches and stations, and glazing bars where they're the local habit. Away from the tropics, walls facing the equator get more and wider windows, and the shady side fewer. Warehouses and halls are often blank, or have a band of windows under the eaves or a corner of offices, with loading doors on the street side. Sheds mostly have none, and garages have up-and-over doors. As the scale shrinks, every scheme steps down the same way: framed windows, then single upright strokes, then one stroke per floor, so blocks of a size look alike whatever their pattern. Towers joined by party walls share one scheme.
- **Front doors** face the street, with a little hood over them, and in southern Europe, France, the Alps and the Low Countries many windows have shutters, drawn in a pen colour.
- **Shop fronts** with wide windows and striped awnings line the ground floors where the map has shops, and along busy and pedestrian streets in town centres.
- **Flat roofs** carry boxes, water tanks (on legs in North America) or rows of solar panels, depending on the region.
- **Balconies** hang off flats where that's the habit: deep ones in southern and eastern Europe and East Asia, and in Paris a continuous railing on the second and fifth floors of the street front.
- **Squares** big enough get a fountain or a statue in the middle. Image-town fountains have oval or polygonal basins, with low arcs of water or jets spilling from a raised bowl.
- **Landmarks the map names**: castles get battlements, and in much of Europe pointed corner turrets; the main hospital buildings get a helipad on the roof; town halls fly a flag. Big works may have a tall chimney stack beside them.
- **Street furniture**: fire stations get a fire engine or two out front and hospitals an ambulance, bus stops get a shelter and a sign, petrol stations a canopy over the pumps, busy junctions traffic lights, and level crossings barriers and a crossbuck. Motorways get crash barriers and gantries of signs. Mountain tops get a summit cross and their height.
- **People** stand about on squares, pedestrian streets and beaches, when the scale is large enough to show them. They, the herds, the boats and ships, the machines in the fields and the sport can be switched off together for a quieter plot.
- **Sport**: football pitches may have a match on (two teams in formation, keepers in goal, a referee) or a training session (a rondo, a shooting drill, a slalom through cones, the coach on the touchline). Tennis courts get singles or doubles, basketball courts a game at one end, and running tracks a race strung out round the lanes or a few runners training. Raceways have a field of single-seaters or GT cars, or karts where the track winds tightly.
- **Roofs** follow what each building is and where it stands. Houses get gabled, hipped, half-hipped or mansard roofs, and L- and T-shaped houses get cross gables. Terraced rows share party walls, with chimney stacks on them. Paris blocks get mansards with dormers. Dutch canal houses turn stepped or neck gables to the street. Factories get sawtooth roofs, churches get a tower and spire (an onion dome in eastern Europe and round the Alps), mosques get a dome and minaret, and temples get tiered roofs.
- **Regional style** is picked from the country: Alpine, Dutch, British, French, Mediterranean, East Asian, North American and more. Each style sets roof shapes, pitches, overhangs, which way ridges run and how many chimneys and dormers there are. You can override it.
- **Traffic** drives in its lanes, on the right or the left depending on the country. How busy a road is depends on its class and how built up the land around it is: a country lane carries the odd car, a town-centre street a steady stream, a motorway is busy anywhere. Vehicles bunch into platoons, queue at red lights near busier junctions, and include buses in town and trucks on main roads. Cars are only parked at the kerb in town, in stretches.
- **Trains** run on a few of the tracks in view, not all of them: each kind of track (main line, yard, tram, metro, narrow gauge, funicular) gets a small budget, trains keep their distance from each other, and one passenger train stands at a platform when there is one. Main lines carry regional units, intercity trains or freight with box, tank, container and open wagons (long, double-stacked freight in North America); yards hold standing wagons; trams, metros and narrow-gauge trains have their own stock. Like the cars, they're drawn in linework only.
- **Aircraft** follow the kind of airfield, read from its label: airliners at the stands of international and regional airports, light singles and twins in rows on small fields' aprons, one aircraft lined up on each runway, and at gliding clubs a glider on tow behind its tug with more gliders waiting beside the strip, one wingtip down. Helipads get a circle and an H, often with a helicopter on them; pads mapped on a building go on its (flat) roof, and hospital roofs sometimes have the air ambulance in. Runway widths follow the airfield's class too.
- **Power lines** are made up, as the map carries none. Transmission lines on lattice pylons cross the country in straight runs laid out on a grid in absolute map coordinates, with supports and complete spans checked against uncropped map geometry. They appear only over mapped land, clear of water, buildings and airfields; uncertain ground and missing supports break the run. An outer tile margin checks continuation, and only runs with both ends beyond the print are drawn. Roadside wires follow connected compatible road sections, bridge small aligned map-seam gaps, keep a consistent side and spacing, and turn at dedicated corner poles. Short village-edge restarts are suppressed. They require clear spans and adjacent supports; isolated poles are omitted. These are artistic inferences, not confirmed utility routes. Wooden poles with sagging wires line roads: in town where that's the custom (the Americas, East Asia, Australia, the tropics), only on country roads elsewhere, with transformer cans here and there. In image mode, neighbouring building counts and footprint areas determine the supply for each settlement. Villages get short roadside poles and larger settlements get taller cross-arm feeders. Each feeder follows a complete street corridor between settlement junctions, road ends or the edge of the sheet. Poles can move along the verge or cross the road to clear an obstruction. Cable risers mark the transition underground. The Power switch controls both modes.
- **Streets** get kerb lines and street lamps, centre dashes and zebra crossings near busy junctions. Main roads may be lined with trees, and roundabouts get a planted island. Parking aisles get painted bays with cars in them.
- **Country road winding** in the Image panel shapes organic lanes between settlements and shore roads, from direct connections to sweeping bends.
- Image-town junctions are controlled as in real places of the style, learnt from OpenStreetMap: roundabouts with planted islands where the roads that meet would have one, traffic lights with stop lines and signalled crossings at busy junctions, and zebra crossings at junctions and along streets as often as real towns paint them. **More roundabouts** in the Image panel turns more junctions into roundabouts than real towns would.
- **Railways** get rails and sleepers; narrow gauge sits closer, and trams run under a contact wire hung from poles. In Europe and East Asia main lines run under overhead wires on masts, and station platforms get a canopy on columns. Where a road or railway goes underground there's a portal with an arch. Footpath dashes stop at parks, woods and grassy ground. Farm tracks have paired wheel ruts and steps have treads.
- **Cable cars, gondolas, chair lifts and drag lifts** hang from pylons, with cabins, chairs or bars along the cable and stations where they end.
- **Airfields** get runway markings, taxiway lines and planes parked on the aprons.
- **Water** has broad ocean crests, fine calm lake ripples and river strokes following the channel, with boats moored along quays and in harbours, sailing boats out on open water, and ferries making crossings. Ships suit the water they're on: container ships, tankers, bulk carriers, cruise ships, coasters, trawlers and tugs at sea (more tugs and trawlers near a harbour), paddle steamers and motor boats on big lakes, barges with hatch covers, containers or an empty hold on wide rivers, tour boats on roomy channels, and small motorboats on narrower rivers. Large vessels need a sustained wide approach, not just enough room for their hull; water depth is not available. Ships under way leave a wake: a calm V behind the stern edged with broken strokes. Bridges over water show their side: masonry arches for most, beams on piers for railways and big roads, and open pier centre lines stand on rows of posts. Closed marina pier footprints get simple deck outlines without added railings. Where industrial land meets the water there are quayside cranes and stacks of containers. Swimming pools get a hard edge and level ripples. Parks are hatched, and parks and woods get trees: palms in town in hot places, cypresses round the Mediterranean.
- **Fields and pitches** each get their own texture:
  - Fields are furrowed along their length, and some are dashed stubble.
  - Vineyards get trellis rows on stakes, and orchards get rows of fruit trees.
  - Meadows get grass tufts, marshes get blue dashes with tufts, and sand is stippled.
  - Villages and suburbs often map only "residential" round their houses, with no gardens drawn. Where buildings cover less than 30% of such an area, the open ground is drawn as lawn with a few garden trees, and each house gets a plot fenced the local way: hedges, walls, fences or picket fences. Front gardens may have a driveway with a car on it, back gardens a shed or a trampoline.
  - Field edges get hedgerows with the odd tree, most of all in Britain and France.
  - Scrub gets bushes, allotments get plots and sheds, bare rock gets boulders and glaciers get crevasses.
  - Beaches get parasols and towels, campsites get pitches of tents, caravans and motorhomes along their lanes (motorhome stops only vans), playgrounds get swings and a slide, and quarries are cut into benches.
  - Open country the map says nothing about gets a light texture and a scattering of trees and bushes in the region's character: dry dots and scrub in deserts, lusher in the tropics, conifers in the north and the mountains.
  - Big barns out among the fields get a silo or two, stubble fields have bales lying in rows, and meadows have small herds: cows in the Alps and central Europe, sheep in Britain, Scandinavia and Australia.
  - Now and then a field is being worked: a tractor ploughing or spreading on ploughed land, a combine harvesting stubble with a tractor and trailer under its auger, or a baler; on a meadow without a herd, a tractor mowing with the cut grass in swaths behind it. Farmyards often have a tractor parked by the barn.
  - Cemeteries get rows of headstones and crosses.
  - Sports pitches get their markings for football, tennis, basketball or a running track, with goals, nets and hoops.
- **Abandonment** (a slider under Town, for maps and images) leaves the town to decay. As the town empties, traffic, trains, aircraft, ships, people, herds and farm work leave street by street, parked cars are driven away or left behind askew as wrecks, half the moored boats are taken away, beaches and campsites empty (caravans are left), and road paint, runway marks and footpaths fade a stretch at a time. Then buildings go derelict, each at its own pace: shutters close, ground-floor windows and doors are boarded, shops are boarded over and lose their awnings, glass breaks, chimneys and rooftop boxes go and holes open in roofs with bare rafters across them. Kerbs break up (never all of them), roads crack and grow weeds, lamps lean and fall, fences rot and fields lie fallow. Houses have a strength tied to their position and seed. A sound house can keep its roof while the house next door collapses, and leave taller walls once its own roof falls. Roofless houses stand as shells, their walls drawn with their thickness and shaded inside, empty window openings in them and their gables still up; churches keep their towers. Then the walls come down in ragged runs, corners standing longest, with rubble heaped along their feet, until only low walls mark the footprints; towers are gutted to frames of floors on columns, losing their top floors, and church towers stand to the end. Fallen tiled roofs lie inside in red heaps, with broken tiles across the floor. Meanwhile nature takes the town back, early and thick by the end: bushes, saplings and then trees in gardens, parks, fallow fields turning to young woodland, the open floors of ruins and the roads, kept clear of the standing walls so the footprints still read, and grass spreading over everything until the lowest walls show only in stretches through it; ivy climbs the ruins, power lines break and hang down, poles fall, sheds collapse, canopies lose their roofs, pitch markings fade and, at the very end, footbridges and bridges on minor roads lose a span. Dry regions keep their ruins higher and grow little; the tropics grow back fast. Which things go, and when, is fixed to the map, and nothing comes back as the slider rises. See [docs/plans/abandonment.md](docs/plans/abandonment.md) for how it works.
- **Shadows** are hatched on the ground. Automatic sunlight comes from geographic south-east in the northern hemisphere and north-east in the southern hemisphere, staying fixed as the view rotates; switch off Automatic sun to choose a direction relative to the view. Walls facing away from the sun have vertical hatching by default, with density determined by their angle to the sunlight rather than their size. Walls nearly parallel to the sunlight stay plain too; turn off Shade walls to omit all wall tone.
- **Cinematic title** (off by default) stands the place name, or any text, in giant 3D block letters on a long straight street between the buildings, as in a film's opening shot. The best street is picked for you: one that crosses the sheet near the middle, wide and built up along both sides. You can pick the next best street, slide and turn the title, set its width, weight, depth, spacing and float, round the letters, shade their sides, clear buildings out of their way, and bring them forward so towers in front don't hide them.
- **Sets of sheets**: split the drawing over 1–4 sheets across and 1–4 down (a triptych, a 2 × 2, a 3 × 3), each printed or plotted on its own and hung side by side with a gap you choose for the frames. The town is drawn once across the whole wall, so a street leaving one sheet carries on in the next exactly where it would be with the margins and gap in between; what would fall there is left out. Each sheet gets its own frame and graticule, the title goes on the bottom-left sheet and the scale bar and north arrow on the bottom-right one. The preview hangs the sheets with their real gaps, Download gives a ZIP of one SVG per sheet, and Pen Plot plots one sheet at a time.
- **Map notes** letter the margin like a printed town map, in single-stroke capitals. The neat line gets a second border with latitude and longitude ticks. Below the drawing are the place name, its coordinates and view, a scale bar, a round north arrow whose needle runs along north as drawn, parallel to north–south streets, and the imprint. Image sheets show the generation seed and an optional creator byline. In the Sheet controls, turn on Frame coordinates and enter a centre latitude and longitude to label the border at the drawing’s scale.

Most buildings aren't tagged with a roof shape in OpenStreetMap, so roofs are worked out from the footprint, the neighbours, the nearest street, points of interest (places of worship, schools), house numbers and land use. Where OpenStreetMap does tag a roof shape or building type, that's used instead. Trees, cars and lamps are made up. Everything made up is tied to the seed and to map position, so the same settings always give the same drawing, and panning doesn't reshuffle it. Click a building in the preview to try another look for it (a terrace changes as a row). It keeps that look through shuffles until you unlock all in the Seed section.

The **Catalogue** in the toolbar lists everything detected, inferred or made up: how each is found, which setting switches it, how small it may get before it is left out, and the regional habits behind the guesses. Against the drawing on show it counts each one and picks out its strokes, which makes it a quick way to review what the inference did.

## Made for pen plotters

- Hidden lines are removed geometrically with a depth buffer before export, so nothing shows through a wall and nothing is drawn twice.
- Each pen is its own Inkscape layer, in plot order: shadows, water, greenery, roofs, linework. Optional custom layer assignments route individual features to any of those five pens.
- Detail can be set per layer too: keep roof hatching sparse while windows stay rich, or thin out trees without losing the ground textures.
- An optional background colour appears in the preview and SVG presentation without adding a filled shape to plot.
- Strokes that touch are joined, and the stroke order is sorted to cut pen-up travel.
- The stats panel shows ink length, pen lifts and an estimated plot time for each pen.
- **Pen Plot** sends the drawing straight to a GRBL pen plotter over USB (Web Serial, in Chrome or Edge), pen by pen, with a prompt to swap pens between layers. It can also download the plot as G-code with a pause before each pen. It is tuned for the UUNA TEK 3.0: GRBL 1.1 at 115200 baud, the pen on a Z axis (Z0 up), origin on the top-left corner of the paper.
- Sizes are real millimetres for A4, A3, A2, Letter, Tabloid and more.

## How it works

| File | Role |
| --- | --- |
| `src/tiles.ts` | Loads OpenFreeMap vector tiles (z14), clips them to tile edges and marks seams |
| `src/osm.ts` | Fetches tagged roof shapes and building types from Overpass, best effort |
| `src/styles.ts` | Regional building styles and offline country detection |
| `src/classify.ts` | Works out each building's kind, height where the map has none, party walls, street side, shop fronts and roof |
| `src/camera.ts` | Orthographic isometric camera: rotation, tilt, scale |
| `src/shape.ts` | Footprint geometry: best-fit rectangles, splitting into rectangles, inset outlines |
| `src/buildings.ts` | Walls and the choice of roof per building, rooftop boxes, cars |
| `src/facades.ts` | Window schemes: picks a pattern and sizes per building, then lays out and draws windows, doors and balconies on each wall |
| `src/growth.ts` | Growth over an abandoned town: bushes and trees taking back gardens, parks, fields, ruins and roads, and ivy on ruined walls |
| `src/ruins.ts` | Ruined buildings for Abandonment: roofless shells with their walls' thickness, gables left standing, ragged walls coming down with rubble at their feet, and towers gutted to frames |
| `src/decay.ts` | Abandonment: phases, when each thing leaves or falls (fixed to the map and never coming back), parked-car fates, faded runs of lines |
| `src/traffic.ts` | Traffic density from road class and surroundings, vehicles in lanes, queues at lights, kerb parking |
| `src/trains.ts` | Joins rail pieces into tracks, picks a few to carry trains, and composes and draws the rolling stock |
| `src/water.ts` | Sea, lake and river hatching, with channel direction from waterways and banks |
| `src/ships.ts` | Ships picked by the kind of water (sea, lake, river, canal), with wakes |
| `src/sport.ts` | Players on pitches and courts, runners on tracks, racing cars and karts on raceways |
| `src/vehicles.ts` | Tractors and their implements, combines, racing cars, fire engines and ambulances |
| `src/aircraft.ts` | Airliners, light aircraft, gliders and helicopters, placed on aprons, runways and helipads by the kind of airfield |
| `src/power-roads.ts` | Connected roadside routes and corner supports |
| `src/power-terrain.ts` | World-space land, obstruction and corridor checks for inferred power routes |
| `src/power.ts` | Conservative inferred power lines: validated land corridors and adjacent roadside poles |
| `src/roofs.ts` | Pitched, mansard, shed and sawtooth roofs, gables, chimneys, dormers, spires, domes |
| `src/streets.ts` | Road surfaces, kerbs, lamps, rails and trams, footpaths, tracks and steps |
| `src/infra.ts` | Lifts, ferries and moored boats, bridges, station platforms, tunnel portals, airfields, road markings, parking bays, roundabouts |
| `src/gardens.ts` | Garden plots and their hedges and fences, driveways, sheds and trampolines, field hedgerows, street trees |
| `src/sites.ts` | Beaches, campsites, playgrounds, farm silos, ports, quarries, fountains and statues, bus shelters, petrol stations, factory chimneys, summit crosses |
| `src/ground.ts` | Field furrows, vineyards, orchards, meadow and marsh symbols, sand, inferred gardens, headstones, pitch markings |
| `src/nature.ts` | Tree placement and tree sprites |
| `src/raster.ts` | Depth buffer, screen-space ground masks, visibility tracing |
| `src/render.ts` | Runs the passes in order |
| `src/lod.ts` | Level of detail: how small each kind of thing may get on paper before it's left out |
| `src/catalogue.ts` | Everything detected, inferred or made up, with how it's found and where its strokes are filed, for the Catalogue |
| `lab/` | The LOD lab: draws test places at several scales and scores wasted ink (`npm run lod`) |
| `test/` | Unit tests and golden drawings from recorded map data (`npm test`, see [docs/testing.md](docs/testing.md)) |
| `src/lettering.ts`, `src/marginalia.ts` | Single-stroke lettering and the map notes in the margin |
| `src/title.ts` | The cinematic title: block letters traced from the single-stroke capitals, extruded and stood on the best straight street |
| `src/humanize.ts` | Deterministic stroke wobble and endpoint variation in sheet millimetres |
| `src/plot.ts` | Stroke joining, ordering, plot stats, SVG output |
| `src/panels.ts`, `src/app/zip.ts` | Sets of sheets: the layout, cutting strokes to each sheet, one SVG per sheet, and the ZIP they download in |
| `src/gcode.ts`, `src/app/grbl.ts` | G-code for GRBL pen plotters, and plotting over USB with Web Serial |
| `src/image.ts`, `src/image-*.ts` | Image mode (in progress): reads a picture, zones the ground to match it, lays out a town on the zones, and scores how like the picture the drawing is. `src/image-masks.ts` holds the local settings and mask geometry, `src/image-pins.ts` the pins. `src/image-tint.ts` blends mask colours into the source picture. Open by invitation for now: `src/app/invite.ts` holds the key |
| `src/worker.ts` | Runs all of the above off the main thread |
| `src/App.tsx`, `src/components/` | The interface, built with React and shadcn/ui (see [DESIGN.md](DESIGN.md)) |

More on how it fits together is in [docs/](docs/README.md).

## Run it locally

```bash
npm install
npm run dev
npm test      # unit tests and golden drawings, offline, see docs/testing.md
npm run lod   # optional: the LOD lab, see docs/lod.md
npm run likeness   # optional: the likeness lab for image mode, see docs/testing.md
```
